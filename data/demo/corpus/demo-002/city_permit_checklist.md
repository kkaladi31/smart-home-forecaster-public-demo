# Minneapolis Home — Building Permit Checklist (Sample)

> SYNTHETIC DOCUMENT. A fictional summary of when a residential building permit is
> commonly required, written for a class capstone demo. It quotes no real municipal
> code and is not legal or code advice. Always confirm with your own building
> department before starting work.

Applies to: **1412 Larkspur Lane, Minneapolis, MN 55409** (the primary home). The
Dallas, TX home is in a different jurisdiction with its own thresholds — do not
apply these to it.

## How to Read This
Three separate questions, and they have three different answers:

1. **Does the city require a permit?** — this document.
2. **Does the HOA require approval?** — the Lakeshore Commons CC&Rs, a separate
   document. ARC approval is not a permit, and a permit is not ARC approval.
3. **Is it safe to do yourself?** — neither document answers that.

Work can need all three, one, or none. A fence, for example, commonly needs HOA
approval and no city permit; a service-panel change needs a permit and is not DIY
work at all.

## Generally Requires a Permit
- Structural work: removing or altering a load-bearing wall, adding or resizing a
  header, altering roof framing, or building a deck over 30 inches above grade.
- Additions, dormers, and enclosing a porch.
- Finishing a basement, or adding or altering any bedroom — egress requirements
  apply to every sleeping room.
- New or relocated plumbing, including moving a sink, adding a bathroom, or
  replacing a water heater.
- Electrical work beyond like-for-like device replacement: new circuits, a panel
  change, a service upgrade, or any subpanel.
- Replacing a furnace or air conditioner, or altering ductwork.
- Reroofing, and window replacement where the opening size changes.
- Retaining walls over four feet, and most work in a drainage or shoreland area.

## Generally Does Not Require a Permit
- Painting, wallpaper, flooring, trim, and cabinetry that does not move plumbing.
- Like-for-like replacement of a light fixture, switch, receptacle, or faucet.
- Countertops, backsplash, and tile.
- Fences under six feet — though the HOA does require approval.
- Sheds under 120 square feet on grade, subject to setbacks.
- Gutter and downspout replacement, heat cable, and roof snow removal.
- Landscaping, decorative rock, and artificial turf, unless grading changes
  drainage or a retaining wall is involved.

## Electrical Permits Are Issued Separately
Electrical work is commonly permitted and inspected through the state electrical
authority rather than the city building counter, which surprises most homeowners.
Applying at the wrong counter is the most common cause of a delayed start.

## Cold-Climate Requirements Worth Knowing
- **Frost depth.** Footings for decks, porches and detached structures must extend
  below the frost line, which is 42 inches at this address. This is the single most
  common reason a deck plan is rejected.
- **Egress windows.** A basement bedroom needs an openable egress window of adequate
  size with a window well; a finished basement without one cannot be called a
  bedroom.
- **Ice-dam remediation.** Adding insulation or improving attic ventilation is
  usually permit-exempt maintenance, but the work often reveals a bath fan venting
  into the attic, and correcting that does require a mechanical permit.
- **Snow load.** Any roof structure change is reviewed against snow load, so a
  cathedral-ceiling conversion or a skylight is a structural review, not a finish.

## Typical Application Steps
1. Describe the scope in writing, with a simple drawing and dimensions.
2. Submit online or at the permit counter with the site plan.
3. Pay the fee, usually based on project valuation.
4. Post the permit where it is visible from the street.
5. Schedule inspections at the required stages — footing, rough-in, insulation, and
   final. Do not cover work before its inspection.

## Working Without a Permit
Unpermitted work commonly triggers a stop-work order, a doubled fee, and a
requirement to uncover finished work for inspection. It also surfaces at resale,
where an unpermitted bedroom or basement finish typically cannot be advertised as
such, and it can give an insurer grounds to contest a related claim.
